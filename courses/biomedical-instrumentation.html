MODULE 1

The ECG & the PQRST Complex

The electrocardiogram is the summed electrical activity of the heart. Each beat is a fixed sequence — P (atrial depolarisation), the sharp QRS (ventricular depolarisation) and T (ventricular repolarisation). The gap between R-peaks (the RR interval) sets the heart rate. The true ECG is only ~1 mV, so any mains pickup is significant.

measured ECGideal (clean)
💡 Try: raise the heart rate — the RR intervals bunch up. Add mains pickup — the fine 50 Hz ripple contaminates the trace, motivating the amplifier & filters in later modules.
Theory — the cardiac cycle & lead voltages

Depolarisation fronts sweeping across the heart create a time-varying dipole; a lead measures its projection. The waves map to events: P = atria, QRS = ventricles (fast, hence tall & narrow), T = ventricular recovery.

\[ \text{Heart rate}=\frac{60}{RR}\ \text{bpm}, \qquad V_{ECG}\sim 0.5\text{–}2\ \text{mV},\ \ f_{content}\approx 0.5\text{–}40\ \text{Hz} \]

Because the signal is tiny and its band overlaps mains harmonics, ECG front-ends need high gain, high input impedance, strong common-mode rejection and careful filtering — the rest of Section 2.

MODULE 2

The Action Potential & "All-or-None"

Every biopotential begins at the cell membrane. A stimulus that depolarises the membrane past its threshold (≈ −55 mV) triggers a full, stereotyped spike — regardless of how much you exceed it. Below threshold, nothing propagates. Sweep the stimulus through threshold and watch the all-or-none law appear.

membrane Vmthreshold −55 mV
💡 Try: increase the stimulus just past the gold threshold line — the response jumps from a small graded bump to a full ~110 mV spike. Bigger stimulus → identical spike. That's "all-or-none".
Theory — Hodgkin–Huxley in one breath

At rest the membrane sits near −70 mV (K⁺-dominated). Depolarisation to threshold opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → explosive influx → upstroke to ≈ +40 mV. Na⁺ channels inactivate and K⁺ channels open → repolarisation, then a brief hyperpolarisation before recovery.

\[ C_m\frac{dV}{dt}= -\big[\,g_{Na}(V-E_{Na})+g_K(V-E_K)+g_L(V-E_L)\,\big]+I_{stim} \]

The regenerative Na⁺ feedback is why the spike is all-or-none, and the refractory period is why signals travel one way and can't tetanise the nerve.

MODULE 3

EEG Rhythms & Frequency Bands

The EEG (~10–100 µV at the scalp) is dominated by rhythmic bands whose mix reflects brain state: δ (0.5–4 Hz, deep sleep), θ (4–8 Hz, drowsy), α (8–13 Hz, relaxed, eyes-closed) and β (13–30 Hz, alert). Slide "arousal" from sleep to alert and watch the rhythm change.

EEG (composite)
💡 Try: at low arousal the trace is slow, large δ waves (deep sleep). Around 0.6 the classic ~10 Hz α rhythm dominates. Near 1.0 it becomes fast, low-amplitude β — an alert brain.
Theory — why bands, and why they matter

Cortical neurons synchronise into population oscillations; the dominant frequency drops as large ensembles synchronise (sleep) and rises as processing desynchronises them (alertness).

\[ x_{EEG}(t)=\sum_{b\in\{\delta,\theta,\alpha,\beta\}} A_b\,\sin(2\pi f_b t+\phi_b) \]

Clinically the band power ratios (e.g. from an FFT — Module 13) drive sleep staging, depth-of-anaesthesia indices and BCI. Amplitudes are tiny, so EEG amps need the highest CMRR and input impedance of any biopotential front-end.

MODULE 4

EMG & the Interference Pattern

Electromyography records the electrical activity of contracting muscle. As you contract harder, the body recruits more motor units and fires them faster, so the summed signal becomes a denser, larger "interference pattern". Its RMS amplitude is the usual measure of effort (used in prosthetics & ergonomics).

surface EMG
💡 Try: at low %MVC the EMG is sparse and small. Push toward 100% — the amplitude and density climb together. That monotonic RMS-vs-force relation is what a myoelectric prosthesis reads.
Theory — motor units & the RMS envelope

A motor unit = one motor neuron + the muscle fibres it drives. Voluntary force rises by recruitment (more units) and rate coding (faster firing). Surface electrodes sum many overlapping motor-unit action potentials into a stochastic, ±few-mV signal (20–500 Hz).

\[ \text{EMG}_{RMS}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} x_i^2}\ \uparrow \ \text{with force} \]

Processing = band-pass (20–450 Hz), rectify, then a moving-average/RMS envelope → a smooth "effort" signal for control.

Section 2 · The acquisition chain
MODULE 5

Electrode–Skin Interface & Motion Artifact

Between metal electrode and tissue sits an electrochemical half-cell that adds a large DC offset (up to hundreds of mV), while movement modulates it into slow baseline wander. The precious ~1 mV ECG rides on top of this. See why the raw electrode signal is unusable without AC-coupling.

raw electrode signal
💡 Try: add offset and motion — the ECG disappears into a slow, wandering baseline. That's why front-ends high-pass at ~0.05–0.5 Hz to strip the DC/wander while keeping the ECG (Module 8).
Theory — the half-cell & the electrode model

An electrode is modelled as a half-cell potential \(E_{hc}\) in series with a parallel R–C (double-layer) and a series resistance. Two electrodes rarely match, so their offsets don't cancel; motion changes the double layer and shifts the baseline.

\[ v_{electrode}(t)=E_{hc}+v_{motion}(t)+v_{bio}(t),\qquad v_{bio}\ll E_{hc} \]

Fixes: Ag/AgCl (non-polarisable, stable), gel to lower impedance, and a high-pass to remove \(E_{hc}\)+wander. But high-pass too aggressively and you distort the low-frequency ST segment — a real clinical trade-off.

MODULE 6

Instrumentation Amplifier & CMRR

Both electrodes pick up the same huge mains field (common-mode, ~1 V) plus a tiny difference (the biosignal). The instrumentation amplifier gives large differential gain but rejects the common-mode by its CMRR. Watch the mains leak into the output collapse as CMRR rises.

amplifier output (input-referred)true ECG
💡 Try: drop CMRR to 40 dB — 1 V of mains leaks through as a huge 50 Hz ripple that swamps the ECG. Raise it to 100+ dB (real ECG amps) and the ECG comes out clean. That single number is why in-amps exist.
Theory — the 3-op-amp in-amp

The classic topology: two buffer amps set the differential gain with one resistor, a difference amp removes the common-mode. Output = differential gain × signal + a leaked fraction of the common-mode set by CMRR.

\[ G=1+\frac{2R}{R_G},\qquad v_{out}=G\!\left(v_{diff}+\frac{v_{cm}}{\text{CMRR}}\right),\qquad \text{CMRR}_{dB}=20\log_{10}\!\frac{A_{diff}}{A_{cm}} \]

90 dB means the common-mode is attenuated ~31 600×. Matched resistors and a right-leg drive (Module 7) push effective CMRR past 100 dB.

MODULE 7

Mains Interference: Notch Filter & Right-Leg Drive

Whatever the amplifier can't reject, a digital notch filter removes — a narrow band-stop at exactly the mains frequency (50 or 60 Hz). A right-leg-drive circuit actively cancels common-mode at the body, shrinking the interference before it's even filtered.

noisy inputafter notch
💡 Try: turn the notch OFF with RLD off — the ECG drowns in mains. Turn RLD on (smaller interference) then the notch on (removes the rest) → clean ECG. Switch 50↔60 Hz to see the notch must match your grid.
Theory — the band-stop biquad

A second-order notch places transmission zeros on the unit circle at ±ω₀ and poles just inside at radius \(r\!\to\!1\), giving a deep, narrow null with everything else passing.

\[ H(z)=\frac{1-2\cos\omega_0\,z^{-1}+z^{-2}}{1-2r\cos\omega_0\,z^{-1}+r^2 z^{-2}},\qquad \omega_0=2\pi f_0/f_s \]

Closer poles (\(r\) near 1) → narrower notch, less ECG distortion, but longer ringing. Note: it only kills the fundamental — harmonics need extra notches, which is why suppressing interference at the source (RLD, shielding) is preferred.

MODULE 8

Biosignal Filtering — the Monitoring Band

The clean-up chain is a band-pass: a high-pass removes electrode drift/baseline wander, a low-pass removes muscle & RF noise. Tune both cutoffs and recover the ECG buried in drift + high-frequency noise.

raw (drift + noise)band-pass output
💡 Try: lower the high-pass to 0.05 Hz — baseline wander creeps back. Raise it to 2 Hz — wander is gone but the ST segment/T wave distort. Lower the low-pass to 15 Hz — noise vanishes but the QRS softens. Every cutoff is a trade-off.
Theory — why a band, and the diagnostic standard

ECG energy lives ≈ 0.5–40 Hz (monitoring) or 0.05–150 Hz (diagnostic). A first-order RC high-pass and low-pass in series give the band; steeper (higher-order) filters trade sharper edges for phase distortion.

\[ f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC},\qquad \text{band-pass}=\text{HP}(f_{hp})\;*\;\text{LP}(f_{lp}) \]

Too-high a high-pass distorts slow features clinicians read (ST elevation); too-low a low-pass smears the QRS. Standards (e.g. AAMI/IEC) fix these limits so diagnoses transfer between machines.

Section 3 · Sensors & transducers
MODULE 9

Wheatstone Bridge — the Pressure Transducer

Most clinical pressures (invasive arterial/venous lines) are sensed by a strain-gauge Wheatstone bridge: pressure flexes a diaphragm, changing gauge resistance by a tiny fraction, which the bridge converts to a proportional voltage. Sweep pressure and read the linear bridge output.

output vs pressureoperating point
💡 Try: the output is a straight line through zero — that linearity is the bridge's gift. Raise excitation for more sensitivity (bigger mV/mmHg) — but real excitation is capped by self-heating & safety.
Theory — bridge balance & gauge factor

A balanced bridge outputs zero. A resistance change \(\Delta R\) unbalances it in proportion. With one active gauge (quarter-bridge):

\[ \frac{\Delta R}{R}=GF\cdot\varepsilon,\qquad V_{out}\approx \frac{V_{ex}}{4}\cdot\frac{\Delta R}{R}\ \ (\propto \text{pressure}) \]

Output is only millivolts, so it feeds an instrumentation amp (Module 6). Using 2 or 4 active gauges multiplies sensitivity and cancels temperature drift.

MODULE 10

Temperature: Thermistor vs RTD

Body/skin temperature is sensed by resistance. An NTC thermistor is very sensitive but exponential (nonlinear); a Pt100 RTD is nearly linear and stable but less sensitive. Pick the sensor and move temperature along its resistance curve.

R–T curveoperating point
💡 Try: on the NTC, resistance plunges with temperature — huge sensitivity but curved. Switch to Pt100 — a gentle straight line: easy to condition, which is why RTDs win where accuracy matters.
Theory — the two resistance laws

NTC follows the β (Steinhart–Hart simplified) law; RTD is a near-linear metal law:

\[ R_{NTC}=R_0\,e^{\beta\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_0}\right)},\qquad R_{RTD}=R_0\,(1+\alpha\,T) \]

Both are read with a bridge or a reference-current + ADC. Thermistors self-linearise over a narrow clinical range (35–42 °C); RTDs are the lab/reference choice.

MODULE 11

Photoplethysmography & Pulse Oximetry (SpO₂)

A pulse oximeter shines red (660 nm) and infrared (940 nm) light through tissue. Oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin absorb the two colours differently, so the ratio of their pulsatile (AC) signals reveals oxygen saturation — completely non-invasively.

red 660 nminfrared 940 nm
💡 Try: drop SpO₂ toward 80% — the red pulsatile signal grows relative to IR (deoxy-Hb absorbs more red), so the ratio R rises. Lower perfusion — both pulses shrink and the estimate gets noisier (why cold fingers read poorly).
Theory — the ratio-of-ratios

Each wavelength's signal has a steady DC (tissue, venous) part and a pulsatile AC (arterial) part. Normalising AC by DC removes path-length; the ratio of the two normalised signals maps to SpO₂ via a calibration curve.

\[ R=\frac{(AC/DC)_{660}}{(AC/DC)_{940}},\qquad \text{SpO}_2 \approx 110-25\,R \]

Because only the pulsatile arterial blood is used, motion and low perfusion are the main error sources — the core signal-processing challenge in oximetry.

MODULE 12

Non-invasive Blood Pressure (Oscillometric)

Automatic cuffs use the oscillometric method: inflate above systolic, then slowly deflate while measuring the tiny pressure oscillations the artery imprints on the cuff. Their amplitude envelope peaks at the mean arterial pressure (MAP); systolic and diastolic are found from fixed fractions of that peak.

cuff pressureoscillations
💡 Try: widen the systolic–diastolic gap — the oscillation "swell" broadens. The oscillations are biggest as the cuff passes MAP; that peak is what the algorithm locks onto first, then estimates SBP/DBP around it.
Theory — envelope detection & the fixed ratios

As cuff pressure falls through the arterial range, each heartbeat produces an oscillation. The peak-to-peak amplitude vs cuff-pressure forms a bell-shaped envelope.

\[ P_{cuff}\!\to\! MAP:\ \text{max oscillation};\quad SBP\approx 0.55\,A_{max}\ (\text{rising}),\ \ DBP\approx 0.85\,A_{max}\ (\text{falling}) \]

Only MAP is measured directly; SBP/DBP come from empirical ratios — which is why oscillometric and auscultatory (Korotkoff) readings can differ, and why cuff size matters.

Section 4 · Processing & safety
MODULE 13

Sampling, Nyquist & ADC Resolution

Digitising a biosignal has two limits. The sample rate must exceed twice the highest frequency (Nyquist) or the signal aliases to a false low frequency; the bit depth sets how finely amplitude is quantised. Break either and the digital record lies.

true signal (8 Hz)sampled & quantised
💡 Try: drop fs below 16 Hz — the 8 Hz signal aliases into a slow wave that isn't there. Drop bits to 3 — the trace turns into coarse steps (quantisation). Both corrupt the record irreversibly.
Theory — Nyquist & quantisation noise

To represent frequencies up to \(f_{max}\) you need \(f_s>2f_{max}\); otherwise energy folds back to \(|f-kf_s|\). An anti-alias analog filter must precede the ADC. Bit depth sets the quantisation step and the noise floor.

\[ f_s>2f_{max},\qquad \Delta=\frac{V_{FS}}{2^{N}},\qquad \text{SNR}_{dB}\approx 6.02\,N+1.76 \]

Typical rates: ECG 250–1000 Hz, EEG 250–512 Hz, EMG 1–2 kHz; resolution 12–24 bits so the small AC signal survives on top of large offsets.

MODULE 14

R-peak Detection, Heart Rate & HRV

Automatic monitors find each heartbeat by detecting the R-peak. A Pan–Tompkins-style chain (differentiate → square → threshold, with a refractory period) marks the beats; the R-to-R intervals give heart rate and its beat-to-beat variation (HRV). Add noise and see false detections appear.

ECGdetected R-peaks
💡 Try: raise noise with a low threshold — spurious red dots appear (false beats), corrupting HR/HRV. Raise the threshold to reject them — but too high and real beats are missed. That's the detector's tuning dilemma.
Theory — the QRS detector & SDNN

The QRS has the steepest slope in the ECG, so differentiating then squaring emphasises it over P/T waves and noise. A threshold + refractory window (~200–300 ms, since beats can't be faster) picks the peaks.

\[ y[n]=\big(x[n]-x[n-2]\big)^2,\qquad HR=\frac{60}{\overline{RR}},\qquad SDNN=\text{std}(RR) \]

HRV (e.g. SDNN) reflects autonomic balance and is used in stress, fitness and cardiac-risk assessment — but it's only as good as the R-peak detector's robustness to noise & ectopics.

MODULE 15

Defibrillator — Capacitive Discharge

A defibrillator charges a capacitor to a high voltage, then discharges it through the chest to depolarise the whole myocardium at once. The delivered energy, peak current and pulse shape are set by the stored charge and the patient's transthoracic impedance.

output voltage
💡 Try: raise the energy — peak voltage/current climb. Increase impedance (a large or dry-skinned patient) — the same energy gives a lower, longer pulse, which is why modern units measure impedance and compensate.
Theory — RC discharge & delivered energy

With capacitor \(C\) charged to \(V_0\) discharging through patient impedance \(Z\), the (monophasic) voltage decays exponentially:

\[ v(t)=V_0e^{-t/\tau},\ \ \tau=ZC,\qquad E=\tfrac12 C V_0^2,\qquad I_{peak}=\frac{V_0}{Z} \]

Biphasic waveforms reverse polarity mid-pulse and defibrillate at lower energy (less myocardial damage). Impedance compensation adjusts voltage/duration so the current — what actually defibrillates — stays effective.

MODULE 16

Patient Safety — Leakage Current & Isolation

The most important number in medical-device design is leakage current — stray current that could flow through the patient. Intact skin tolerates milliamps (macroshock), but a catheter or pacing lead delivers current straight to the heart, where just ~10 µA can trigger fibrillation (microshock). Slide the current and see where it lands.

hazard thresholdsyour leakage
💡 Try: on "skin", ~1 mA is just perceptible and ~100 mA fibrillates. Switch to "catheter to heart" — the whole scale shifts: the danger line drops to ~10 µA. That 10,000× difference is why isolated (floating, type CF) inputs exist.
Theory — macroshock, microshock & isolation

Effect depends on current, path and duration. Through skin: ~1 mA perception, ~10–16 mA "let-go" (can't release), ~100 mA ventricular fibrillation. Directly to the heart, current density is enormous, so the fibrillation threshold collapses to ~10–100 µA.

\[ I=\frac{V}{Z_{body}},\qquad \text{isolation} \Rightarrow Z\uparrow,\ I\downarrow;\qquad \text{limits: IEC 60601 (µA range)} \]

Defences: galvanic isolation of patient-connected circuits, low leakage design, protective earthing, equipotential bonding, and type CF (cardiac-floating) applied parts for direct-to-heart connections.
