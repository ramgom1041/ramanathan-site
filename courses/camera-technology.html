How a Camera Works — Photon → Pixel

A camera is a measurement instrument. Light is focused by the lens, converted to charge in the sensor, amplified, digitised by the ADC, then processed by the ISP into an image. Step through the pipeline.

MODULE 3

The Exposure Triangle

Three controls set image brightness — aperture (how wide the lens opens), shutter speed (how long light is collected), and ISO (sensor gain) — but each has a side-effect: depth of field, motion blur, and noise. Balance them for a correct exposure.

💡 Try: open the aperture (small f-number) → brighter but shallow depth of field. To keep exposure constant, use a faster shutter or lower ISO. That's the photographer's trade-off.
Theory
\[ \text{EV} = \log_2\!\frac{N^2}{t},\qquad \text{Brightness} \propto \frac{t\cdot \text{ISO}}{N^2} \]

\(N\) = f-number, \(t\) = exposure time. Each "stop" doubles or halves the light: one stop of aperture (×√2 in N), one stop of shutter (×2 in t), and one stop of ISO are interchangeable for brightness — but not for side-effects.

SECTION 2 — Optics & Lenses

MODULE 4

Field of View & Focal Length

Focal length and sensor size set how much of the world the camera sees. A short focal length (wide lens) captures a broad angle; a long one (telephoto) zooms into a narrow slice. Crucial for choosing a machine-vision lens to cover a given target at a given distance.

💡 Try: short focal length → wide angle, large field covered but lower detail. Long focal length → narrow angle, the target fills the frame (more pixels on target).
Theory
\[ \text{FOV}=2\arctan\!\frac{d_{sensor}}{2f},\qquad W_{scene}=2\,D\tan\!\frac{\text{FOV}}{2}=\frac{D\cdot d_{sensor}}{f} \]

So the field width at the target scales with sensor size and working distance, and inversely with focal length. The "spatial resolution" (mm per pixel) = field width ÷ pixel count.

MODULE 5

Depth of Field

Only a slice of the scene is acceptably sharp. That slice — the depth of field — grows with a narrower aperture (bigger f-number), a shorter focal length, and a farther subject. The shaded band shows what's in focus.

💡 Try: f/22 + 35 mm → almost everything sharp (landscape). f/1.4 + 200 mm on a close subject → a razor-thin focus slice (portrait bokeh).
Theory
\[ H=\frac{f^2}{N\,c}+f,\qquad D_n=\frac{s(H-f)}{H+s-2f},\qquad D_f=\frac{s(H-f)}{H-s} \]

\(H\) is the hyperfocal distance; focusing there makes everything from \(H/2\) to infinity sharp. \(c\) is the circle of confusion (≈0.03 mm for full-frame). When \(s\ge H\), the far limit is infinity.

MODULE 6

Lens Technologies

The core machine-vision lens question: what focal length covers my target at my working distance? Set the sensor, the distance, and the field you must image — the required focal length is computed, along with the lens category and notes on prime/zoom/macro/telecentric.

💡 Try: a small target far away → long focal length (telephoto). A big field up close → short focal length (wide). For precise dimensional measurement, a telecentric lens removes perspective error entirely.
Theory
\[ f=\frac{\text{sensor width}\times \text{working distance}}{\text{field width}},\qquad \text{magnification}=\frac{\text{sensor width}}{\text{field width}} \]
  • Prime (fixed f): sharper, cheaper, used in most vision systems. Zoom: flexible f but more distortion.
  • Macro: for magnifications near or above 1×. Wide/fisheye: large FOV, heavy distortion.
  • Telecentric: constant magnification regardless of object distance → no perspective error, ideal for gauging & 3D metrology.
MODULE 7

Lens Distortion & Calibration

Real lenses bend straight lines. Barrel distortion (wide lenses) bulges the image outward; pincushion (telephoto) pinches it inward. Camera calibration estimates these coefficients so machine-vision measurements stay accurate. Watch a square grid warp.

distortedideal grid
💡 Try: k₁ < 0 → barrel (lines bow outward, like a GoPro). k₁ > 0 → pincushion. k₁ = 0 → perfect rectilinear grid. Calibration finds and undoes this.
Theory
\[ r_{d}=r\,(1+k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + \dots) \]

Radial distortion displaces each point along its radius from the optical centre by a polynomial in \(r\). The Brown–Conrady model (used by OpenCV's calibrateCamera) solves for \(k_1,k_2,k_3\) plus tangential terms, then undistort reverses it.

SECTION 3 — Image Sensors

MODULE 8

Sensor Formats & Pixel Size

Sensor size drives image quality more than megapixels. A bigger sensor gathers more light (less noise, more dynamic range) and changes the effective field of view via the crop factor. For a fixed megapixel count, a bigger sensor also means larger pixels — better sensitivity. Compare formats to scale.

selectedother formats
💡 Try: pick a tiny 1/2.3" sensor at 48 MP → minuscule pixels (noisy in low light). A full-frame at 24 MP → big pixels (clean). Same MP, very different image quality.
Theory
\[ \text{crop factor}=\frac{43.27\text{ mm}}{\text{sensor diagonal}},\qquad \text{pixel pitch}=\frac{\text{sensor width}}{\text{horizontal pixels}} \]

The crop factor multiplies a lens's effective focal length (a 25 mm lens on a 2/3" sensor frames like a ~100 mm lens on full-frame). Larger pixels collect more photons → higher signal-to-noise and dynamic range, which is why sensor area, not megapixels, is the headline spec for low-light and metrology cameras.

MODULE 9

CCD vs CMOS Sensor Architecture

Two ways to read charge off a sensor. A CCD shifts every pixel's charge, bucket-brigade style, to one corner amplifier — uniform but slow. A CMOS sensor amplifies and digitises at each pixel/column in parallel — fast, low-power, and now dominant. Switch between them to see the readout path.

💡 Most machine-vision & phone cameras today are CMOS (speed, cost, on-chip ADC, region-of-interest readout). CCDs survive in niches needing very uniform, low-noise, high-full-well imaging (some scientific/astro).
Theory & comparison

How they differ

  • CCD: photo-charge is transferred pixel-to-pixel to a single output amplifier, then digitised off-chip. Very uniform (one amplifier → low fixed-pattern noise), high full-well, but slower, higher power, and prone to blooming and smear.
  • CMOS: each pixel has its own amplifier; ADCs sit per-column on-chip. Massively parallel → high frame rate, low power, windowed (ROI) readout, cheaper. Historically noisier, but modern CMOS rivals CCD.
  • Shutter: CCDs are typically global shutter; most CMOS are rolling shutter (see Module 5), though global-shutter CMOS exists.

Key metrics either way: quantum efficiency (QE), full-well capacity, read noise, dark current, and dynamic range (Module 13).

MODULE 10

Global vs Rolling Shutter

A global shutter exposes every pixel at once. A rolling shutter (most CMOS sensors) reads rows top-to-bottom over a short time — so a fast-moving object is captured at slightly different positions per row, producing skew. Critical for fast machine-vision inspection.

global (true)rolling capture
💡 Try: fast object + long readout → the rectangle becomes a slanted parallelogram (the classic "jello" / skew). Zero speed or zero readout → no distortion (matches global shutter).
Theory

Row \(y\) is exposed at time \(t(y)=\dfrac{y}{H}T_{read}\). An object moving at velocity \(v\) shifts by \(v\,t(y)\) by the time that row is read, so a vertical edge tilts by \(\Delta x = v\,T_{read}\) over the full frame height — a shear, not a simple blur. Global shutters avoid it but cost more and can have lower full-well capacity.

MODULE 11

Dynamic Range & Noise

How much tonal range can a sensor capture, and how clean is it? Dynamic range = the ratio of the brightest signal (full-well) to the noise floor (read noise). SNR grows with light until the pixel saturates. These set how usable an image is in shadows and highlights.

SNR (dB)saturation
💡 Try: bigger full-well + lower read noise → wider dynamic range (more stops). The SNR curve rises at √(signal) in the shot-noise region, then clips at full-well. Bit depth should be enough to not waste the range.
Theory
\[ \text{DR}_{dB}=20\log_{10}\!\frac{N_{full}}{\sigma_{read}},\quad \text{DR}_{stops}=\log_2\!\frac{N_{full}}{\sigma_{read}},\quad \text{SNR}(S)=\frac{S}{\sqrt{S+\sigma_{read}^2}} \]

Noise sources: shot noise \(\sqrt{S}\) (fundamental, from photon statistics), read noise (electronics, sets the floor), dark current (thermal), and fixed-pattern noise. In bright light, SNR is shot-limited (\(\sqrt{S}\)); in shadows, read-noise-limited. Bit depth should at least resolve the noise floor — more bits than the DR warrants just stores noise.

SECTION 4 — Image Science

MODULE 12

Bayer Pattern & Colour Imaging

A sensor pixel only counts photons — it's colour-blind. To get colour, most cameras put a colour filter array over the pixels (the Bayer RGGB mosaic), then demosaic — interpolate the two missing channels at every pixel. Toggle between the raw mosaic and the reconstructed colour image.

💡 Untick "demosaiced" to see the raw RGGB mosaic — every pixel holds just one colour. Tick it to see the interpolated full-colour result. Notice green has twice as many pixels (the eye is most sensitive to green / luminance).
Theory

The Bayer CFA uses a 2×2 tile of R, G, G, B — 50% green, 25% red, 25% blue — because green carries most luminance detail. Demosaicing reconstructs the missing channels by interpolation (bilinear → edge-aware methods like Malvar or AHD).

  • Trade-off: a colour sensor loses spatial resolution & can show moiré / false colour at fine detail.
  • Alternatives: 3-CCD/prism (full RGB per pixel), Foveon (stacked), or monochrome sensors (no CFA → maximum resolution & sensitivity — often preferred in machine vision).
MODULE 13

Resolution, MTF & Nyquist

"Megapixels" isn't resolution. Real resolving power is the MTF — how much contrast survives at each spatial frequency (line pairs/mm). The lens MTF and the sensor's pixel pitch both limit it; beyond the sensor's Nyquist frequency, fine detail aliases into moiré.

system MTFlensNyquist
💡 Try: smaller pixels → higher Nyquist (more resolution) but only if the lens can deliver it. A soft lens (low MTF50) becomes the bottleneck — "the lens out-resolves the sensor, or vice-versa".
Theory
\[ f_{Nyquist}=\frac{1}{2\,p}\ \text{(lp/mm, p in mm)},\qquad \text{MTF}_{sys}=\text{MTF}_{lens}\times\text{MTF}_{sensor} \]

The pixel aperture gives a sensor MTF of \(|\text{sinc}(f p)|\). Anything above \(f_{Nyquist}\) can't be sampled correctly and folds back as aliasing/moiré — which is why some cameras add an optical low-pass (anti-alias) filter. System MTF is the product of every element's MTF, so the weakest link dominates.

MODULE 14

Image Quality Metrics

Quantify "good image" with engineering numbers, not the eye: sharpness, contrast, SNR, dynamic range, colour accuracy. Degrade a test pattern and watch the metrics move.

💡 Add noise → SNR drops. Add blur → MTF50 drops. These are the numbers spec sheets quote (EMVA 1288, ISO 12233).
Theory & objectives
Objectives: measure sharpness/SNR/contrast objectively.
\[ SNR_{dB}=20\log_{10}(\mu/\sigma),\quad C=\frac{I_{max}-I_{min}}{I_{max}+I_{min}} \]
Common mistakes: judging by eye; relying on a single metric.

SECTION 5 — 3D & Advanced Imaging

MODULE 15

Stereo Vision

Two cameras a known baseline apart see a point at slightly different image positions — the disparity. From it you recover depth by triangulation. This is the principle behind stereo vision and 3D measurement systems.

camerasmeasured point
💡 Try: small disparity → far object; large disparity → near. A wider baseline B improves depth precision (but shrinks the overlapping field of view).
Theory
\[ Z=\frac{f\cdot B}{d} \]

Depth \(Z\) is inversely proportional to disparity \(d\): nearby points shift a lot between the two views, distant points barely move. Depth precision degrades with distance (\(\partial Z/\partial d \propto Z^2\)), which is why a larger baseline or longer focal length helps for far targets.

Beyond stereo: structured light, Time-of-Flight, laser triangulation and LiDAR. Each trades range vs accuracy vs speed vs ambient-light robustness. Compare them.

SECTION 6 — Industrial Camera Systems

MODULE 18

Camera Interfaces & Data Rates

A camera's resolution × bit depth × frame rate sets its data rate — which dictates the interface (USB3, GigE, Camera Link, CoaXPress…). Pick the specs and see the required bandwidth and the cheapest interface that can carry it. (Plus: the CCD-vs-CMOS trade-off below.)

💡 Try: 5 MP @ 60 fps, 12-bit → a few hundred MB/s, needs USB3/GigE-Vision class. Crank to 25 MP @ 100 fps → only Camera Link / CoaXPress can keep up.
Theory
\[ \text{data rate} = \text{pixels} \times \frac{\text{bits}}{8} \times \text{fps} \]

CCD vs CMOS: CCD historically gave lower noise & global shutter but is slower and power-hungry; modern CMOS dominates — fast, low-power, on-chip ADC, and now excellent noise (with global-shutter variants for motion). Interface ballpark throughput: USB2 ≈ 40, GigE ≈ 110, USB3 ≈ 380, Camera Link ≈ 850, 10-GigE ≈ 1100, CoaXPress (CXP-12 ×4) ≈ 5000 MB/s.

MODULE 20

Machine-Vision System Design & Timing

A working system synchronises camera + lens + lighting + trigger + processing. Build the timing budget: acquisition + transfer + processing must fit inside the cycle time, or you miss parts.

💡 Push processing past the cycle budget → the bar turns red: parts are missed. Use a hardware trigger & strobe synced to exposure.
Theory & objectives
Objectives: budget acquisition→transfer→processing within the cycle.
\[ t_{cycle}=t_{exp}+t_{read}+t_{proc},\quad \text{throughput}=1/t_{cycle} \]
Common mistakes: no hardware trigger; ignoring transfer/processing latency; under-spec PC.
Quiz: (1) Why encoder trigger for line-scan. (2) Cycle-time terms. (3) Strobe–exposure sync.
