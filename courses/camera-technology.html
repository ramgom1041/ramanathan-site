MODULE 1

How a Camera Works — Photon → Pixel

A camera is a measurement instrument. Light is focused by the lens, converted to charge in the sensor, amplified, digitised by the ADC, then processed by the ISP into an image. Step through the pipeline.

💡 Drag the stage from 1→5 to follow light becoming a digital image. Each block adds noise & latency — the weakest stage caps quality.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

A camera is a measurement chain, not a magic box. Light from the scene is collected by the lens, converted to electric charge in the sensor, amplified, digitised, then processed into pixels. Every stage adds noise, latency and potential distortion — so the final quality is set by the weakest stage, never by megapixels alone.

Stage by stage

  • Lens: forms the optical image; sets resolution (MTF), light gathering (f-number) and geometric accuracy (distortion).
  • Photosite: the photoelectric effect converts photons to electrons. Quantum efficiency (QE) is the fraction captured; charge collects in the full-well.
  • Readout / CDS amplifier: reads the charge and applies gain; correlated double sampling removes reset noise. This stage's residual is the read noise floor.
  • ADC: quantises voltage to digital numbers; bit depth sets the number of levels.
  • ISP: black-level subtraction, demosaic, white balance, gamma, denoise, sharpen, compress.
\[ N_e = QE \cdot N_{photons}, \qquad DN = g\,N_e + \text{offset}, \qquad SNR_{shot} = \sqrt{N_e} \]

Worked example

1000 photons hit a pixel with QE = 0.7 → 700 electrons. Shot-noise-limited SNR ≈ √700 ≈ 26 (≈ 28 dB), before read noise is added.

In practice (machine vision)

For measurement, prefer raw/linear output and switch off sharpening, gamma and lossy compression — they alter pixel values and bias gauging. Know where each correction happens so you can disable what corrupts data.

Common pitfalls

Equating megapixels with quality; ignoring QE and lens MTF; assuming the ISP is lossless.

Quick check

(1) Order the stages. (2) Where is demosaicing performed? (3) What does the ADC fundamentally limit?

MODULE 2

Light & the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Different wavelengths reveal different physics. Choose the band that makes your feature visible — and note the sensor & optics each one requires.

💡 Slide into SWIR → silicon sensors stop working (need InGaAs). Slide into LWIR → thermal, no illumination needed, Ge optics.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Choose the waveband where your feature has contrast. Materials reflect, absorb, transmit and emit differently across the spectrum, so a defect invisible in colour can be obvious in UV, NIR or thermal.

The physics

Photon energy \(E=hc/\lambda\): shorter wavelength = higher energy. Silicon responds ~350–1100 nm (so visible + NIR work on ordinary sensors); SWIR needs InGaAs; thermal (MWIR/LWIR) uses InSb or microbolometers and senses emitted radiation (no lamp needed). Hot bodies radiate per Planck's law with a peak given by Wien.

\[ E=\frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad \lambda_{max}=\frac{2898}{T[K]}\ \mu m\ \text{(Wien)}, \qquad R(\lambda)=\frac{I_{sample}}{I_{reference}} \]

Band cheat-sheet

  • UV (200–400 nm): fluorescence, fine surface defects, semiconductor.
  • Visible (400–700): colour, general inspection, ANPR.
  • NIR (700–1000): sees through some inks/skin, veins, biometrics — on silicon.
  • SWIR (1–2.5 µm): water/moisture content, through-silicon, plastic sorting.
  • MWIR/LWIR (3–14 µm): emissive thermal imaging; needs Germanium optics.
  • X-ray: internal density — BGA voids, castings, weld inspection.

Worked example

A 300 K object peaks at \(2898/300 \approx 9.7\,\mu m\) → squarely LWIR, which is why room-temperature thermal cameras are LWIR.

Pitfalls

Using visible light when the feature only shows in another band; glass optics in thermal (opaque — use Ge).

Quick check

(1) Band for water content? (2) Detector for LWIR? (3) Wien peak of 300 K.

MODULE 3

The Exposure Triangle

Three controls set image brightness — aperture (how wide the lens opens), shutter speed (how long light is collected), and ISO (sensor gain) — but each has a side-effect: depth of field, motion blur, and noise. Balance them for a correct exposure.

💡 Try: open the aperture (small f-number) → brighter but shallow depth of field. To keep exposure constant, use a faster shutter or lower ISO. That's the photographer's trade-off.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Exposure = how many electrons fill the well. In machine vision we set it deterministically (fixed time/gain + controlled light), never "auto". Three knobs — aperture, exposure time, gain — set brightness, each with a side effect.

The three controls

  • Aperture (f-number \(N=f/D\)): light \(\propto 1/N^2\); also sets depth of field and diffraction.
  • Exposure time \(t\): light \(\propto t\); but a moving object blurs if it crosses ≥1 pixel during \(t\).
  • Gain (ISO): multiplies signal and noise — it brightens without adding information.
\[ EV=\log_2\frac{N^2}{t}, \quad \text{Brightness}\propto \frac{t\cdot\text{gain}}{N^2}, \quad t_{max}<\frac{p\cdot M}{v_{obj}}\ (\text{blur}<1\,px) \]

Worked example

Object at 0.5 m/s, imaged at 20 µm/px (so 1 px = 20 µm on the object): \(t_{max} < 20\,\mu m / 0.5\,m/s = 40\,\mu s\). You need strong, possibly strobed, light to expose that fast.

In practice

Always prefer more light + low gain over high gain (which destroys SNR). Use flicker-free or strobed illumination synced to the trigger; keep exposure within the line/frame period.

Pitfalls

Brightening with gain instead of light; exposure too long for the motion/line-rate.

Quick check

(1) What causes motion blur? (2) Gain vs light for SNR? (3) Compute EV at f/8, 1/125 s.

SECTION 2 — Optics & Lenses

MODULE 4

Field of View & Focal Length

Focal length and sensor size set how much of the world the camera sees. A short focal length (wide lens) captures a broad angle; a long one (telephoto) zooms into a narrow slice. Crucial for choosing a machine-vision lens to cover a given target at a given distance.

💡 Try: short focal length → wide angle, large field covered but lower detail. Long focal length → narrow angle, the target fills the frame (more pixels on target).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Focal length and sensor size decide how much scene you see and how many pixels land on the target. Lens selection is the first hard number in any vision system.

\[ FOV=2\arctan\frac{d_{sensor}}{2f}, \quad W_{scene}=\frac{D\cdot d_{sensor}}{f}, \quad f\approx\frac{d_{sensor}\cdot D}{W_{scene}}, \quad \text{mm/px}=\frac{W_{scene}}{N_{px}} \]

Worked example

Cover a 200 mm-wide part at WD = 600 mm on a 2/3″ sensor (8.8 mm wide): \(f \approx 8.8\times600/200 \approx 26\,mm\) → pick the nearest standard (25 mm) and re-check the exact FOV. With 2448 px across, mm/px ≈ 200/2448 ≈ 0.082 mm.

In practice

Always confirm the lens image circle covers the sensor format and the lens resolves the pixel pitch (Module 13). "Pixels on the smallest feature" should be ≥ 3 for reliable detection/measurement.

Pitfalls

Ignoring sensor size; confusing FOV with resolution; lens image circle too small (vignetting/dark corners).

Quick check

(1) f for a given FOV/WD. (2) Effect of a larger sensor at fixed f. (3) Define mm/px.

MODULE 5

Depth of Field

Only a slice of the scene is acceptably sharp. That slice — the depth of field — grows with a narrower aperture (bigger f-number), a shorter focal length, and a farther subject. The shaded band shows what's in focus.

💡 Try: f/22 + 35 mm → almost everything sharp (landscape). f/1.4 + 200 mm on a close subject → a razor-thin focus slice (portrait bokeh).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Only a slice of the scene is acceptably sharp — the depth of field (DoF). It's bounded by the circle of confusion (CoC, \(c\)): the largest blur spot still seen as a point.

\[ H=\frac{f^2}{N c}+f, \quad D_n=\frac{s(H-f)}{H+s-2f}, \quad D_f=\frac{s(H-f)}{H-s}\ (s

Drivers

DoF grows with a larger f-number (smaller aperture), shorter focal length, and greater distance. Focusing at the hyperfocal \(H\) makes everything from \(H/2\) to ∞ sharp.

The diffraction limit

Stopping down too far softens the whole image: the diffraction spot \(\approx 2.44\lambda N\) eventually exceeds the pixel. On small-pixel MV sensors this can start around f/5.6–f/8 — so there's an optimum aperture, not "smaller is always sharper".

Worked example

\(f=50\,mm, N=8, c=0.03\,mm\): \(H \approx 50^2/(8\times0.03)+50 \approx 10.5\,m\). Focus there and ~5.2 m → ∞ is sharp.

Pitfalls

Forgetting diffraction; assuming telecentric lenses obey ordinary DoF rules; tiny apertures to "get everything sharp".

Quick check

(1) DoF vs aperture. (2) Meaning of hyperfocal. (3) When is the far limit ∞?

MODULE 6

Lens Technologies

The core machine-vision lens question: what focal length covers my target at my working distance? Set the sensor, the distance, and the field you must image — the required focal length is computed, along with the lens category and notes on prime/zoom/macro/telecentric.

💡 Try: a small target far away → long focal length (telephoto). A big field up close → short focal length (wide). For precise dimensional measurement, a telecentric lens removes perspective error entirely.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Lens type matters as much as focal length. Machine vision favours fixed-focal industrial lenses and, for metrology, telecentric optics.

Lens families

  • Prime / fixed: sharp, stable — preferred in MV (no zoom/focus drift).
  • Wide / normal / telephoto: short → wide FOV; long → narrow FOV, more pixels on target.
  • Macro: magnification ≥ 1:1 for tiny parts.
  • Telecentric: entrance pupil at infinity → constant magnification with distance and no perspective error — essential for accurate gauging of 3D parts.
\[ \text{Entocentric: } M=\frac{f}{WD-f}\ (M\ \text{changes with WD}); \qquad \text{Telecentric: } M=\text{const} \]

Why telecentric for metrology

With an ordinary (entocentric) lens, parts farther away look smaller — a stepped object measures wrong. A telecentric lens images parallel rays, so a feature's size is independent of its height/position. The catch: the front element must be ≥ the object size, so they're bulky and costly.

In practice

Match resolution (lp/mm) to the sensor; verify mount (C/CS/F/M12) and image circle; avoid consumer zooms.

Pitfalls

Entocentric lenses for measurement; telecentric on objects bigger than its aperture.

Quick check

(1) Why telecentric for gauging? (2) Define macro. (3) What to check besides focal length.

MODULE 7

Lens Distortion & Calibration

Real lenses bend straight lines. Barrel distortion (wide lenses) bulges the image outward; pincushion (telephoto) pinches it inward. Camera calibration estimates these coefficients so machine-vision measurements stay accurate. Watch a square grid warp.

distortedideal grid
💡 Try: k₁ < 0 → barrel (lines bow outward, like a GoPro). k₁ > 0 → pincushion. k₁ = 0 → perfect rectilinear grid. Calibration finds and undoes this.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Real lenses bend straight lines. For measurement you must model and remove distortion via calibration, or every pixel-to-mm conversion is biased.

Types

  • Radial — barrel (wide lenses bulge out) and pincushion (tele pinch in).
  • Tangential — from lens/sensor misalignment.
  • Perspective — from a tilted optical axis (keystone).
\[ r_d = r\,(1+k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6), \qquad K=\begin{bmatrix}f_x&0&c_x\\0&f_y&c_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

Calibration

Image a known target (checkerboard) from many poses; solve for the intrinsics \(K\), distortion coefficients, and per-view extrinsics (Zhang's method, OpenCV calibrateCamera). Quality is judged by reprojection error (aim < 0.3 px). Then undistort rectifies images before measuring.

In practice

Spread calibration views across the whole FOV and tilt angles; recalibrate if the lens/focus changes. Telecentric lenses still need calibration for residual distortion.

Pitfalls

Skipping calibration before gauging; too few or poorly distributed target views; changing focus after calibrating.

Quick check

(1) Sign of \(k_1\) for barrel. (2) What \(K\) contains. (3) What reprojection error tells you.

MODULE 8

Sensor Formats & Pixel Size

Sensor size drives image quality more than megapixels. A bigger sensor gathers more light (less noise, more dynamic range) and changes the effective field of view via the crop factor. For a fixed megapixel count, a bigger sensor also means larger pixels — better sensitivity. Compare formats to scale.

selectedother formats
💡 Try: pick a tiny 1/2.3" sensor at 48 MP → minuscule pixels (noisy in low light). A full-frame at 24 MP → big pixels (clean). Same MP, very different image quality.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Sensor "format" (optical size) and pixel pitch drive resolution, sensitivity and lens choice. Bigger pixels gather more light (better SNR/dynamic range); more/smaller pixels give finer sampling — you can't maximise both at a fixed sensor size.

\[ \text{crop factor}=\frac{43.27}{d_{diag}}, \quad p=\frac{w_{sensor}}{N_{px,horiz}}, \quad \text{full-well}\propto p^2 \]

Format ladder

1/4″ → 1/3″ → 1/2.3″ → 2/3″ → 1″ → 4/3″ → APS‑C → full-frame (36×24). Crop factor scales an equivalent focal length and the diffraction-limited aperture.

Worked example

5 MP on a 1/2″ (6.4 mm) sensor → ~2560 px wide → p ≈ 2.5 µm. The same 5 MP on 2/3″ (8.8 mm) → p ≈ 3.4 µm: bigger pixels, ~1.9× the area, better low-light SNR.

In practice

Choose pixel size for the required SNR/light budget first, then resolution; confirm the lens covers the format and out-resolves the pitch.

Pitfalls

Chasing megapixels at the cost of pixel size (and SNR); a lens whose image circle is too small.

Quick check

(1) Crop factor of a 1″ sensor. (2) Pixel pitch from MP. (3) Bigger pixels → ?

MODULE 9

CCD vs CMOS Sensor Architecture

Two ways to read charge off a sensor. A CCD shifts every pixel's charge, bucket-brigade style, to one corner amplifier — uniform but slow. A CMOS sensor amplifies and digitises at each pixel/column in parallel — fast, low-power, and now dominant. Switch between them to see the readout path.

💡 Most machine-vision & phone cameras today are CMOS (speed, cost, on-chip ADC, region-of-interest readout). CCDs survive in niches needing very uniform, low-noise, high-full-well imaging (some scientific/astro).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Two architectures move charge off the chip differently — which changes speed, noise, uniformity and cost.

CCD

Charge is shifted bucket-brigade style through the array to a single output amplifier, then digitised off-chip. One amplifier → excellent uniformity (low fixed-pattern noise) and high full-well, but it's slow, power-hungry, and prone to blooming (overflow) and smear.

CMOS

Each pixel has its own amplifier and ADCs sit per column, so the whole array reads out in parallel → high frame rate, low power, region-of-interest (windowed) readout and low cost. Modern CMOS (BSI, global-shutter Pregius) rivals CCD noise and dominates machine vision.

\[ \text{Read time}_{CCD}\propto N_{px}\ (\text{serial}), \qquad \text{Read time}_{CMOS}\propto N_{rows}\ (\text{parallel columns}) \]

In practice

Pick CMOS for speed, ROI, cost and integration; CCD survives in niches needing extreme uniformity or very high full-well (some scientific/astronomy). Always note the shutter type (Module 10).

Pitfalls

Assuming CCD is always lower noise today; ignoring rolling-vs-global shutter; forgetting host bandwidth for fast CMOS.

Quick check

(1) Why is CMOS faster? (2) Cause of CCD smear. (3) Benefit of ROI readout.

MODULE 10

Global vs Rolling Shutter

A global shutter exposes every pixel at once. A rolling shutter (most CMOS sensors) reads rows top-to-bottom over a short time — so a fast-moving object is captured at slightly different positions per row, producing skew. Critical for fast machine-vision inspection.

global (true)rolling capture
💡 Try: fast object + long readout → the rectangle becomes a slanted parallelogram (the classic "jello" / skew). Zero speed or zero readout → no distortion (matches global shutter).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

The shutter sets when each pixel integrates light. With motion this is critical.

Global vs rolling

Global shutter: every pixel starts and stops exposure simultaneously → moving objects are captured undistorted. Rolling shutter: rows are exposed/read sequentially top-to-bottom, so a fast object is at a different position per row → skew/shear ("jello"), and partial exposure with flash.

\[ t(\text{row }y)=\frac{y}{H}T_{read}, \qquad \Delta x_{skew}=v\cdot T_{read} \]

Worked example

Object at 2 m/s, readout 10 ms → a vertical edge shifts 20 mm top-to-bottom of the frame: severe shear. A global shutter (or a very short strobe ≪ T_read with global reset) avoids it.

In practice

Use global shutter for moving parts, robotics, drones and triggered inspection. Rolling shutter is fine for static scenes and is cheaper / sometimes lower-noise. A strobe alone doesn't fully fix rolling shutter unless the sensor supports global reset.

Pitfalls

Rolling shutter on fast motion; expecting a flash to "freeze" rolling-shutter skew.

Quick check

(1) Cause of skew. (2) When is global mandatory? (3) Strobe + rolling caveat.

MODULE 11

Dynamic Range & Noise

How much tonal range can a sensor capture, and how clean is it? Dynamic range = the ratio of the brightest signal (full-well) to the noise floor (read noise). SNR grows with light until the pixel saturates. These set how usable an image is in shadows and highlights.

SNR (dB)saturation
💡 Try: bigger full-well + lower read noise → wider dynamic range (more stops). The SNR curve rises at √(signal) in the shot-noise region, then clips at full-well. Bit depth should be enough to not waste the range.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Dynamic range (DR) is the ratio of the largest signal a pixel can hold (full-well) to the noise floor (read noise). SNR describes how clean a given signal is. Together they tell you whether you'll see detail in both highlights and shadows.

\[ DR_{dB}=20\log_{10}\frac{N_{full}}{\sigma_{read}}, \quad DR_{stops}=\log_2\frac{N_{full}}{\sigma_{read}}, \quad SNR(S)=\frac{S}{\sqrt{S+\sigma_{read}^2+\sigma_{dark}^2}} \]

Noise sources

  • Shot noise \(\sqrt{S}\): fundamental photon statistics — sets the ceiling in bright light.
  • Read noise: electronics; sets the floor / shadow performance.
  • Dark current: thermal, grows with exposure & temperature (cool for long exposures).
  • Fixed-pattern noise: pixel-to-pixel offset/gain variation (PRNU/DSNU).

Worked example

Full-well 20 ke⁻, read noise 4 e⁻ → DR = 20·log₁₀(5000) ≈ 74 dB ≈ 12.3 stops. Max SNR ≈ √20000 ≈ 141 (≈ 43 dB) at saturation.

In practice

Bit depth should at least resolve the noise floor — more bits than the DR warrants just stores noise. Use EMVA 1288 numbers to compare sensors fairly.

Pitfalls

Over-specifying bit depth; ignoring cooling for long exposures; comparing DR quoted with different methods.

Quick check

(1) DR formula. (2) Shot- vs read-noise regimes. (3) Why \(\sqrt{S}\)?

MODULE 12

Bayer Pattern & Colour Imaging

A sensor pixel only counts photons — it's colour-blind. To get colour, most cameras put a colour filter array over the pixels (the Bayer RGGB mosaic), then demosaic — interpolate the two missing channels at every pixel. Toggle between the raw mosaic and the reconstructed colour image.

💡 Untick "demosaiced" to see the raw RGGB mosaic — every pixel holds just one colour. Tick it to see the interpolated full-colour result. Notice green has twice as many pixels (the eye is most sensitive to green / luminance).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

A pixel only counts photons — it has no colour sense. A colour filter array (CFA) over the pixels plus demosaicing reconstructs colour, at a cost in resolution and sensitivity.

The Bayer mosaic

A 2×2 tile of R,G,G,B — 50% green because the eye (and luminance detail) is most sensitive there. Demosaicing interpolates the two missing channels at every pixel; better algorithms (Malvar, AHD) are edge-aware to limit zippering.

\[ Y=0.299R+0.587G+0.114B, \qquad (R,G,B)\to(g_rR,\,G,\,g_bB)\ \text{(white balance)} \]

Mono vs colour for MV

A monochrome sensor has no CFA → ~full spatial resolution, ~3× sensitivity, and no demosaic artifacts / false colour. For most inspection and all NIR/UV work, mono is the better engineering choice; use colour only when colour is the feature.

In practice

Calibrate white balance to the illuminant; beware moiré/false colour on fine periodic detail; consider 3‑CCD/prism for full-RGB-per-pixel.

Pitfalls

Using colour where mono is better; ignoring the illuminant; expecting full resolution from a Bayer sensor.

Quick check

(1) Why twice as many green? (2) Mono advantages. (3) What demosaicing estimates.

MODULE 13

Resolution, MTF & Nyquist

"Megapixels" isn't resolution. Real resolving power is the MTF — how much contrast survives at each spatial frequency (line pairs/mm). The lens MTF and the sensor's pixel pitch both limit it; beyond the sensor's Nyquist frequency, fine detail aliases into moiré.

system MTFlensNyquist
💡 Try: smaller pixels → higher Nyquist (more resolution) but only if the lens can deliver it. A soft lens (low MTF50) becomes the bottleneck — "the lens out-resolves the sensor, or vice-versa".
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

"Megapixels" is sampling, not resolving power. The honest measure is the MTF — the contrast a system preserves at each spatial frequency (line pairs/mm). The system MTF is the product of lens and sensor MTFs.

\[ f_{Nyquist}=\frac{1}{2p}, \quad MTF_{sensor}=|\text{sinc}(f p)|, \quad MTF_{sys}=MTF_{lens}\times MTF_{sensor} \]

Nyquist & aliasing

A sensor with pitch \(p\) can only sample frequencies up to \(f_{Nyquist}=1/2p\). Detail above it can't be represented and folds back as aliasing / moiré — irreversible. Some cameras add an optical low-pass (anti-alias) filter to suppress it at a small sharpness cost.

Worked example

Pixel pitch 3.45 µm → \(f_{Nyquist}=1/(2\times0.00345)\approx 145\) lp/mm. To exploit it, the lens must still deliver useful contrast at ~145 lp/mm; a soft lens becomes the bottleneck.

In practice

Match lens and sensor: a high-MP sensor behind a mediocre lens wastes pixels; a superb lens on a coarse sensor is sampling-limited. Spec lenses by MTF at the sensor's Nyquist.

Pitfalls

Buying resolution the lens can't deliver; ignoring aliasing on periodic targets.

Quick check

(1) Nyquist from pitch. (2) System-MTF rule. (3) What causes aliasing.

MODULE 14

Image Quality Metrics

Quantify "good image" with engineering numbers, not the eye: sharpness, contrast, SNR, dynamic range, colour accuracy. Degrade a test pattern and watch the metrics move.

💡 Add noise → SNR drops. Add blur → MTF50 drops. These are the numbers spec sheets quote (EMVA 1288, ISO 12233).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

"Looks good" isn't a spec. Image quality is a set of measurable metrics you can put in a requirement and test against (EMVA 1288, ISO 12233).

Key metrics

  • Sharpness: MTF50 / acutance (from a slanted-edge test).
  • SNR: \(20\log_{10}(\mu/\sigma)\) on a flat patch.
  • Contrast: \((I_{max}-I_{min})/(I_{max}+I_{min})\).
  • Dynamic range (Module 11) and colour accuracy (ΔE).
  • Uniformity (shading), defective pixels.
\[ SNR_{dB}=20\log_{10}\frac{\mu}{\sigma}, \qquad \Delta E=\sqrt{\Delta L^2+\Delta a^2+\Delta b^2} \]

In practice

Define pass/fail on the metric that limits your task — e.g. MTF50 for fine-feature reading, SNR for low-contrast defect detection, ΔE for colour grading. Measure on standard targets under controlled light.

Pitfalls

Judging by eye; using one metric for everything; testing under uncontrolled lighting.

Quick check

(1) SNR in dB. (2) What MTF50 means. (3) What ΔE measures.

SECTION 5 — 3D & Advanced Imaging

MODULE 15

Stereo Vision

Two cameras a known baseline apart see a point at slightly different image positions — the disparity. From it you recover depth by triangulation. This is the principle behind stereo vision and 3D measurement systems.

camerasmeasured point
💡 Try: small disparity → far object; large disparity → near. A wider baseline B improves depth precision (but shrinks the overlapping field of view).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Two cameras a known baseline \(B\) apart see a point at slightly different image columns; that disparity \(d\) gives depth by triangulation.

\[ Z=\frac{f\,B}{d}, \qquad \Delta Z\approx\frac{Z^2}{f\,B}\,\Delta d \]

Geometry

After rectification, corresponding points lie on the same row (epipolar line), so matching is a 1-D search. Nearby points have large disparity; far points almost none — so depth precision degrades as \(Z^2\).

Worked example

\(f=800\,px, B=120\,mm, d=40\,px\) → \(Z=800\times120/40=2400\,mm\). A ±0.5 px matching error gives \(\Delta Z\approx Z^2\Delta d/(fB)\approx 30\,mm\) at 2.4 m — improve it by widening \(B\) or using a longer \(f\).

In practice

Textured surfaces are needed for correspondence (or add a projected pattern → structured light). Wider baseline = better far-range precision but smaller overlap and more occlusion.

Pitfalls

Ignoring the \(Z^2\) error growth; textureless/shiny surfaces; poor rectification.

Quick check

(1) \(Z=?\) (2) How does error scale with range? (3) Why is texture required?

MODULE 16

3D Imaging Technologies

Beyond stereo: structured light, Time-of-Flight, laser triangulation and LiDAR. Each trades range vs accuracy vs speed vs ambient-light robustness. Compare them.

💡 Compare structured light vs ToF: high accuracy short range vs fast full-field at longer range.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Several ways to capture 3D, each a different trade of range, accuracy, speed, cost and ambient-light robustness.

Methods

  • Passive stereo: two cameras + matching; cheap, needs texture.
  • Structured light: project known patterns (stripes/speckle), decode deformation → very accurate at short range; struggles in bright ambient.
  • Time-of-Flight (ToF): measure light's round-trip time/phase per pixel → fast, full-field, modest accuracy.
  • Laser triangulation: a laser line + angled camera → sub-mm profilometry, ideal for line-scan on conveyors.
  • LiDAR: scanning/flash ToF for long-range mapping (automotive, mobile robots).
\[ \text{ToF pulse: } Z=\frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}, \qquad \text{ToF CW: } Z=\frac{c\,\phi}{4\pi f_{mod}} \]

In practice

Sub-mm metrology on a conveyor → laser triangulation or structured light; fast obstacle sensing → ToF; large-area mapping → LiDAR; cheap depth with texture → stereo. Watch ambient light and specular/transparent surfaces.

Pitfalls

ToF for sub-mm gauging; structured light outdoors; stereo on textureless parts.

Quick check

(1) ToF equation. (2) Best for sub-mm? (3) LiDAR use case.

MODULE 17

Multispectral & Hyperspectral Imaging

Capture many spectral bands to discriminate materials by their signature — bruised fruit, vegetation stress, coatings — that look identical in RGB. Switch bands to reveal hidden features.

💡 Toggle to NIR → a bruise invisible in RGB appears as a dark patch (the basis of food sorting).
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Materials have spectral signatures. Capturing several bands (multispectral) or hundreds (hyperspectral) lets you classify materials and find defects invisible in RGB.

\[ R(\lambda)=\frac{I_{sample}(\lambda)}{I_{ref}(\lambda)}, \qquad NDVI=\frac{NIR-Red}{NIR+Red} \]

Multispectral vs hyperspectral

Multispectral: a few discrete bands (e.g. RGB+NIR) — cheap, fast, enough when you know the bands. Hyperspectral: hundreds of contiguous bands → a spectral "cube" (x, y, λ); usually acquired push-broom (line by line). Powerful but heavy on data and calibration.

Applications

Agriculture (crop health via NDVI), food sorting (bruises, sugar/moisture), recycling/plastic ID, pharma, art & remote sensing.

In practice

Use the fewest bands that separate your classes; always reference-calibrate against a white/dark standard and a known illuminant.

Pitfalls

Hyperspectral when 2–3 bands suffice; ignoring illuminant/calibration; underestimating data volume.

Quick check

(1) NDVI formula. (2) Multi vs hyper. (3) What is push-broom?

SECTION 6 — Industrial Camera Systems

MODULE 18

Camera Interfaces & Data Rates

A camera's resolution × bit depth × frame rate sets its data rate — which dictates the interface (USB3, GigE, Camera Link, CoaXPress…). Pick the specs and see the required bandwidth and the cheapest interface that can carry it. (Plus: the CCD-vs-CMOS trade-off below.)

💡 Try: 5 MP @ 60 fps, 12-bit → a few hundred MB/s, needs USB3/GigE-Vision class. Crank to 25 MP @ 100 fps → only Camera Link / CoaXPress can keep up.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

The interface must carry the camera's sustained data rate at the needed cable length and CPU budget. Compute the rate first, then choose.

\[ \text{rate} = W\times H\times \text{bit depth}\times \text{fps}\ \ (\text{bits/s}) \]

The options

  • USB3 Vision ~350–400 MB/s, ≤ ~3–5 m, plug-and-play.
  • GigE Vision ~110 MB/s, ≤ 100 m, PoE, multi-camera friendly.
  • 10GigE ~1.1 GB/s, long reach.
  • Camera Link ~850 MB/s (Full), short, needs a frame grabber.
  • CoaXPress (CXP-12) multi-GB/s over coax, long reach — high-end.
  • MIPI CSI-2 for embedded/board-level (very short).

Worked example

5 MP × 8-bit × 60 fps = 5e6×8×60 ≈ 2.4 Gb/s ≈ 300 MB/s → USB3 Vision works at short range; for 20 m you'd move to 10GigE or CoaXPress.

In practice

Account for cable length, host bandwidth/CPU, number of cameras, and sustained vs burst rate. PoE simplifies GigE wiring.

Pitfalls

Ignoring cable length; forgetting host/PCIe bandwidth; quoting burst not sustained.

Quick check

(1) Data-rate formula. (2) GigE limit. (3) Longest-reach interface.

MODULE 19

Illumination Systems

Lighting is often the highest-leverage decision in machine vision — "good lighting beats more pixels". Each geometry makes different features pop. See a scratched part under each.

💡 Switch bright→dark field → surface scratches flip from invisible to bright. Backlight → perfect silhouette for gauging.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Lighting is usually the highest-leverage decision in machine vision — "good lighting beats more pixels". The goal is to maximise contrast of the feature and suppress everything else, including ambient.

Geometries

  • Backlight: silhouette → edges, dimensions, presence/absence.
  • Bright field (frontal): general; flat features.
  • Dark field (low-angle): scratches, edges, engravings glow on a dark background.
  • Dome / diffuse: shadowless — tames specular and curved parts.
  • Coaxial (on-axis): flat shiny surfaces, mirror-like parts.
  • Structured / line: 3D profiling.
\[ E\propto \frac{1}{d^2}, \qquad C=\frac{I_{feature}-I_{background}}{I_{feature}+I_{background}}\ \text{(maximise)} \]

Colour & polarisation

Pick a wavelength that the part reflects/absorbs to boost contrast (and filter ambient). Polarisers kill specular glare. Strobing/over-driving freezes motion and overpowers ambient.

In practice

Solve contrast with light, not software; control or exclude ambient; avoid specular hotspots with diffuse/dome.

Pitfalls

Fixing in software what lighting should solve; uncontrolled ambient; specular blow-out.

Quick check

(1) Dark field reveals? (2) Polariser use. (3) Backlight is for?

MODULE 20

Machine-Vision System Design & Timing

A working system synchronises camera + lens + lighting + trigger + processing. Build the timing budget: acquisition + transfer + processing must fit inside the cycle time, or you miss parts.

💡 Push processing past the cycle budget → the bar turns red: parts are missed. Use a hardware trigger & strobe synced to exposure.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

A working system is a synchronised pipeline: trigger → strobe + exposure → readout/transfer → processing → result. Everything must fit inside the cycle time or parts are missed.

\[ t_{cycle}=t_{exposure}+t_{readout}+t_{processing}, \qquad \text{throughput}=\frac{1}{t_{cycle}}, \qquad \text{line rate}=\frac{v}{\Delta y} \]

Triggering & sync

Use a hardware trigger (sensor/PLC) for deterministic timing; for line-scan, trigger from an encoder so line rate tracks conveyor speed. Strobe must fire within the exposure window.

Worked example

30 parts/s → 33 ms cycle. If exposure 3 ms + transfer 8 ms + processing 12 ms = 23 ms, you fit (and have margin). Push processing to 25 ms → 36 ms total → you drop parts.

In practice

Budget acquisition + transfer + processing with margin; consider edge/GPU processing for heavy algorithms; design for vibration, heat and IP rating.

Pitfalls

No hardware trigger; ignoring transfer/processing latency; under-spec PC.

Quick check

(1) Why encoder trigger for line-scan? (2) Cycle-time terms. (3) Strobe–exposure sync.

MODULE 21

Camera Selection Workflow (Wizard)

Turn application requirements into a complete spec. Enter your object, smallest feature, working distance, accuracy and speed — get recommended resolution, pixel size, lens focal length, frame rate, shutter and interface.

💡 Tighten the smallest feature 2× → required resolution (and cost) jumps. Add object speed → it recommends a global shutter.
📖 Theory class (detailed)

Concept

Selection is a workflow, not a guess. Start from the application requirements and derive every component in order.

The workflow

  1. Resolution: \(N_{px}=\dfrac{\text{object size}}{\text{smallest feature}}\times(\ge 3\ \text{px/feature})\).
  2. Sensor & pixel size: from resolution + SNR/light budget.
  3. Lens: from FOV, WD, required mm/px; check MTF & image circle.
  4. Frame/line rate: from part speed/throughput.
  5. Shutter (global if moving), mono/colour, waveband.
  6. Interface: from data rate + cable length.
  7. Illumination: to maximise feature contrast.
  8. Validate DR/SNR, environment, budget.
\[ \text{mm/px}=\frac{\text{FOV}}{N_{px}}, \quad f\approx\frac{d_{sensor}\cdot WD}{\text{object}}, \quad \text{rate}=W H\,b\,\text{fps} \]

Worked example

200 mm object, 0.2 mm smallest feature, 3 px/feature → ~3000 px across (~6–9 MP depending on aspect). Moving on a belt → global shutter; compute data rate → choose interface; pick lighting for the defect type.

Pitfalls

Choosing the camera before defining the smallest feature & accuracy; forgetting lighting and interface in the budget.

Quick check

(1) Resolution from feature size. (2) When global shutter. (3) First step of the workflow.
